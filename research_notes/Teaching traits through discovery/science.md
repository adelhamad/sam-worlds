# Science teaching contract: traits and adaptations for a seven-year-old

## 1. What is the relationship between traits, adaptations, and inheritance?

### Takeaway
Teach adaptations as a subset of traits, never as the opposite of traits. Separate two questions: where a characteristic comes from, and what evidence shows about its function in a particular environment.

### Cited Findings
- Berkeley defines an adaptation as a feature that arose and was favored by natural selection for its current function; adaptations help survival and/or reproduction in the current environment. They can be anatomical, behavioral, or biochemical—not only visible body parts. It explicitly says that not all traits are adaptations. — [UC Museum of Paleontology, Understanding Evolution](https://evolution.berkeley.edu/evolution-101/mechanisms-the-processes-of-evolution/adaptation/)
- NGSS says **many**, not all, characteristics are inherited. Grade 3 heredity assessment emphasizes nonhuman organisms, parent/offspring patterns, and variation; genetic mechanisms and predicting traits are outside its assessment boundary. — [3-LS3-1](https://www.nextgenscience.org/pe/3-ls3-1-heredity-inheritance-and-variation-traits)
- NGSS explicitly includes environmental influences ranging from diet to learning, and says many characteristics involve both inheritance and environment. Its concrete example is normally tall plants becoming stunted with insufficient water. — [3-LS3-2](https://www.nextgenscience.org/pe/3-ls3-2-heredity-inheritance-and-variation-traits)
- Berkeley's criteria include heritability, actual function, fitness benefit, and evolutionary history. Simply performing a helpful function today is insufficient to establish an adaptation for that function. — [Qualifying as an adaptation](https://evolution.berkeley.edu/qualifying-as-an-adaptation/)

### Inferences
Recommended implementation contract, derived from the sources above:
1. **Never require a single choice between “Trait” and “Adaptation.”** “Both a trait and an adaptation” must be accepted for verified examples. Prefer a nested “Adaptations” area inside “Traits,” or sequential observation and explanation activities.
2. Do not define *trait* as “something you are born with,” or *adaptation* as “something learned to survive.” Use “inherited trait” when inheritance is specifically intended. Physical appearance alone does not identify inheritance.
3. Avoid the false rule “body part = inherited, behavior = learned.” For the first game, explicitly scope the content: “Today we are investigating body features.” That keeps tasks simple without claiming all traits are body features.
4. Keep provenance and function separate in content data: observation, inheritance/environment evidence, habitat, function, supporting evidence, source URL, and adaptation status (`supported`, `not established`, or a specifically justified non-adaptation). Do not infer adaptation status automatically from a helpful-function flag.
5. A learned action can help an animal survive without that individually learned action being an inherited evolutionary adaptation. The broader capacity to learn is a different trait; avoid conflating these levels.

Suggested child-facing wording (instructional paraphrases, not source quotations):
- **Trait:** “A characteristic of a living thing. Shape, color, and size are examples.”
- **Inherited:** “Passed from parents to their young.” Follow with “Young living things can be like their parents without being exactly the same.”
- **Adaptation:** “An inherited trait shaped over many generations that helps living things survive or have young in their environment.” Add the subset sentence: “An adaptation is a kind of trait.”
- **Environment:** “The surroundings where a living thing lives.”
- **Evidence:** “What we observe or find out that supports an idea.”
- **Function:** “What a part does.”

### Gaps
- These are recommended simplifications, not experimentally validated wording for this particular child. NGSS does not mandate an exact seven-year-old definition of adaptation.
- “Trait” varies in breadth across teaching resources. Use the broader NGSS-compatible meaning consistently; do not switch midway to an exclusively genetic definition.

## 2. What evidence and examples are safe for the game?

### Takeaway
Let the child discover a feature–function–environment relationship, then supply a sourced explanation of adaptation. Do not turn an attractive illustration, a guessed benefit, or success in a toy simulation into proof of evolutionary history.

### Cited Findings
- Berkeley identifies leaf mimicry in a Costa Rican katydid as an adaptation for evading predators and bat echolocation as an adaptation for catching insects. — [Adaptation](https://evolution.berkeley.edu/evolution-101/mechanisms-the-processes-of-evolution/adaptation/)
- Blood's redness is a by-product of chemistry, rather than itself an adaptation; the chemistry may be adaptive. Berkeley also distinguishes neutral variation, historical accidents, outdated adaptations, and exaptations. — [Not an adaptation](https://evolution.berkeley.edu/misconceptions-about-natural-selection-and-adaptation/not-an-adaptation/)
- Berkeley warns that the basic form of feathers may be an exaptation for flight: feathers existed in nonflying ancestors. Present function alone does not settle the historical question. — [Qualifying as an adaptation](https://evolution.berkeley.edu/qualifying-as-an-adaptation/)
- NGSS Grade 3 asks for evidence that different organisms survive differently **in a particular habitat**; evidence can include organisms' needs, characteristics, and habitat conditions. — [3-LS4-3](https://www.nextgenscience.org/pe/3-ls4-3-biological-evolution-unity-and-diversity)
- Grade 1 explicitly uses turtle shells as protective structures, roots as stabilizing structures, and human-made equipment modeled on natural structures. — [1-LS1-1](https://www.nextgenscience.org/pe/1-ls1-1-molecules-organisms-structures-and-processes)

### Inferences
Recommended contrasting cards and what their answer keys may safely claim:

| Card / supplied clues | Safe conclusion and feedback | Source basis |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Leaf-like katydid among leaves; predator-search scene; scientist fact card | “Its leaf-like appearance is a trait **and** an adaptation. Blending in can help it avoid predators.” Have the player identify the matching-background evidence. | [Berkeley adaptation](https://evolution.berkeley.edu/evolution-101/mechanisms-the-processes-of-evolution/adaptation/) |
| The same katydid on leaves versus a plain contrasting background | “The same trait can be more helpful in one setting than another.” A background change does not instantly erase its evolutionary status as an adaptation. Ask about hiding **here**, rather than whether it has stopped being an adaptation. | [NGSS habitat context](https://www.nextgenscience.org/pe/3-ls4-3-biological-evolution-unity-and-diversity) |
| A turtle shell versus a child's bicycle helmet | “Both can protect. The shell is a body feature; the helmet is human-made equipment.” This contrasts usefulness with biological inheritance, rather than traits versus adaptations. | [NGSS structure/function examples](https://www.nextgenscience.org/pe/1-ls1-1-molecules-organisms-structures-and-processes) |
| Same kind of plant, one with enough water and one with insufficient water; growth record supplied | “Height is a trait. Water conditions can affect it.” Do not label the shorter plant's height an adaptation merely because it grew in a dry situation. | [NGSS environmental influence](https://www.nextgenscience.org/pe/3-ls3-2-heredity-inheritance-and-variation-traits) |
| Fictional story: a dog is explicitly taught to respond to a new hand signal | “This action was learned through practice.” Label the scenario as a story; its training record, not an inference from the dog's movement, establishes the answer. | [NGSS learning as environmental influence](https://www.nextgenscience.org/pe/3-ls3-2-heredity-inheritance-and-variation-traits) |
| A mammal's red blood, with a scientist explanation | “The red color is a trait, but the color itself is not an adaptation.” This is a sourced non-adaptation example; optional extension because the chemistry is abstract for age seven. | [Berkeley non-adaptations](https://evolution.berkeley.edu/misconceptions-about-natural-selection-and-adaptation/not-an-adaptation/) |
| A fictional animal with purple spots and no further evidence | “We can describe the spots. We need more clues to know whether they help it survive here.” Never equate missing evidence with a proven non-adaptation. | [Berkeley evidence criteria](https://evolution.berkeley.edu/qualifying-as-an-adaptation/) |

**Recommended sorting wording:**
- Observation round: “Body feature” / “Learned action in this story” / “Human-made item.” Choose cards that unambiguously fit; explain that this round sorts descriptions, not adaptations.
- Function round: “Helps with this challenge” / “Does not help with this challenge” / “Need more clues.” Always specify the challenge and environment.
- Relationship round: place known examples inside a large **Traits** collection; mark verified examples **Also an adaptation**. Leave uninvestigated traits labeled **Not enough evidence yet**, not “ordinary trait” or “trait only.”
- If using an explicit final three-way classification, use “Trait and adaptation” / “Trait, but not an adaptation for this function” / “Need more evidence.” Reserve the middle label for genuinely sourced cases.

**Feedback contract:** name the observation, mechanism, and context: “You spotted the leaf-like shape. Among leaves, it can be harder for predators to notice.” Follow with “Scientists identify this as an adaptation.” A game demonstration supports the hiding mechanism; it does not independently demonstrate natural selection.

### Gaps
- This research does not establish adaptation status for arbitrary color, stripe, fur, beak, or foot examples in the existing app. Each specific species/function claim needs its own reliable source before being made a scored fact.
- Avoid generic “feathers evolved for flight,” “all camouflage works everywhere,” and “every useful feature is an adaptation.” Do not use human eye color, earlobes, or tongue rolling as convenient “non-adaptation” answer keys without separate evidence.

## 3. What is age-appropriate, and what should the rebuild assess?

### Takeaway
Build around observing, comparing, and connecting features to needs. Explicit inheritance/adaptation language is an accessible extension toward Grade 3, not the central Grade 2 NGSS performance expectation.

### Cited Findings
- Grade 2 NGSS 2-LS4-1 asks children to observe plants and animals and compare diversity in different habitats; it does not assess memorizing specific species names in specific habitats. — [2-LS4-1](https://www.nextgenscience.org/pe/2-ls4-1-biological-evolution-unity-and-diversity)
- Grade 1 foundations connect external body parts with meeting needs and solving problems through modeled structures. — [1-LS1-1](https://www.nextgenscience.org/pe/1-ls1-1-molecules-organisms-structures-and-processes)
- Berkeley's K–2 guide emphasizes foundations for later evolutionary understanding and avoiding misconceptions rather than expecting formal evolution instruction at this age. — [K–2 teaching guide](https://evolution.berkeley.edu/teach-evolution/teaching-guides/teaching-guides-k-2/)
- Berkeley's K–2 “Form and Function” activity asks students to select a plant or animal part and indicate how it supports that organism's needs. — [Form and Function](https://evolution.berkeley.edu/teach-resources/form-and-function/)

### Inferences
Recommended acceptance criteria for the implementation PR:
1. The learner can point to and describe a visible characteristic before hearing its label.
2. The learner can connect one verified feature to a need using “This helps ___ because ___.” A correct label with no supporting clue is not the entire learning goal.
3. At least one encounter tests the same feature in different settings, with context visible while answering.
4. At least one encounter shows something useful that is not an inherited body adaptation (helmet or explicitly taught action).
5. At least one “Need more clues” response is genuinely correct and rewarded. Unknown is distinct from false.
6. A final relationship question accepts “An adaptation is also a trait”; no screen, badge, tutorial, or answer key contradicts this.
7. Explain through short spoken/visible sentences and concrete images. Keep “natural selection,” “fitness,” “exaptation,” and genetic mechanisms in teacher notes rather than required child vocabulary.
8. If showing change over time, use generations and existing variation. Do not animate an individual acquiring a new inherited body part because it needs one, chooses one, or earns an upgrade. A habitat-matching puzzle may choose an organism/feature to investigate, but should not pretend that this is how evolution occurs.
9. Prefer nonhuman family examples and do not require the child to compare their own appearance with caregivers; this also matches Grade 3 assessment scope.
10. Record scientific source URLs alongside content, and describe standards alignment as “Grade 1–2 foundations with Grade 3 extensions,” rather than claiming the game by itself fulfills all these performance expectations.

### Gaps
- No child usability testing or learning-outcome experiment was performed. Interaction pacing, reading load, retention, and accessibility need testing in the rebuilt game.
- Research scope is scientific content and teaching contract only; no application code was changed. The coordinating implementation session should carry these requirements into its new PR.
