# Rebuild TraitSafari Around Evidence Through Play

**Keep one world with nested concepts: every adaptation is a trait, but not every trait is an adaptation.** Rebuild TraitSafari as a connected detective journey: notice a feature, investigate its effect in a particular place, and construct an evidence story. Separate “Traits” and “Adaptations” worlds risk presenting mutually exclusive categories; use activity stations within one world instead. Make animal names incidental and replace four-choice recall with touchscreen comparisons, constructions, and predictions. Teach concrete structure–function relationships first, then introduce inheritance and generations in short illustrated explanations. These are evidence-informed design recommendations, not a validated curriculum for this particular app ([Berkeley](https://evolution.berkeley.edu/evolution-101/mechanisms-the-processes-of-evolution/adaptation/)).

## One world makes the relationship explicit

Use a large **Traits collection containing an Adaptations pocket**, with known examples visibly belonging to both. Ask children to identify both memberships; spatial nesting alone is insufficient. Child-facing wording can be: “A trait is a characteristic of a living thing,” and “An adaptation is an inherited trait shaped over many generations that helps living things survive or have young in their environment.” Present those ideas in separate short moments. Scope the opening journey to body features without claiming that all traits or adaptations are visible structures: adaptations can also be behavioral or physiological ([Berkeley](https://evolution.berkeley.edu/evolution-101/mechanisms-the-processes-of-evolution/adaptation/)).

This targets **Grade 1–2 foundations with Grade 3 extensions**, rather than claiming seven-year-olds must master formal evolutionary mechanisms. Grade 1 connects structures with needs; Grade 3 asks for evidence-based habitat arguments. A picture-storybook study with children aged 5–8 supports the possibility of learning and generalizing causal evolutionary explanations, but does not establish the effectiveness of this touchscreen design ([NGSS 1-LS1-1](https://www.nextgenscience.org/pe/1-ls1-1-molecules-organisms-structures-and-processes); [NGSS 3-LS4-3](https://www.nextgenscience.org/pe/3-ls4-3-biological-evolution-unity-and-diversity); [Kelemen et al., 2014](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24503874/)).

## Three connected activities replace trivia with investigation

**Collect a feature.** Show a clear photograph and large feature cards. Children tap a card and place it in their collection, distinguishing an organism’s characteristic from its surroundings. Start with the documented leaf-like katydid example. After investigating its hiding function, reveal a sourced scientist card and add “Also an adaptation.” Uninvestigated features remain “We need more clues,” not “not an adaptation” ([Berkeley](https://evolution.berkeley.edu/evolution-101/mechanisms-the-processes-of-evolution/adaptation/)).

**Build a habitat comparison.** Let children place leafy or plain backgrounds behind the same insect, predict where it will be harder to notice, then inspect both scenes side by side. Hold size, pose, and lighting constant; change only the background. An outline reveal can support comparison. Label this a model of hiding, not measured survival, and avoid invented survival percentages. Emphasize: “The place changed. The body shape stayed the same.” Creative choice concerns arranging a test, not upgrading an animal’s body.

**Compose an evidence story.** Children assemble picture tiles into “leaf-shaped body → harder to notice → among leaves,” then choose the observation supporting their explanation. Offer plausible combinations rather than a prefilled single route. Model one explanation, remove support for retrieval, and introduce a new case with all necessary facts. Optional drawing or adult-directed speech adds expression but must not automatically earn understanding credit. Combining visual and verbal representations and asking explanatory questions follow learning-science guidance; the exact activities remain design hypotheses ([IES practice guide](https://ies.ed.gov/ncee/wwc/practiceguide/1)).

Use tap-select/tap-place as the default, optional dragging, large controls of at least 48px, keyboard equivalents, visible focus, and text-plus-icon cues. Present one brief instruction at a time. Package images, credits, case data, and any audio for offline use. Avoid timers and speed rewards.

## Assess relationships, transfer, and evidence after correction

Record separate evidence for noticing a trait, including an adaptation within traits, linking feature–effect–environment, predicting a changed-context outcome, and transferring to an unfamiliar case. Include later retrieval before hints. A provisional mastery rule is independent inclusion and causal linking on two cases, one changed-context prediction, and one later fresh-case retrieval; **this threshold is a product decision requiring child testing**. Correct labels or tile placement alone are insufficient proof of freely generated understanding ([NGSS 3-LS4-3](https://www.nextgenscience.org/pe/3-ls4-3-biological-evolution-unity-and-diversity); [IES](https://ies.ed.gov/ncee/wwc/practiceguide/1)).

Track first committed responses, help shown, case identity, and transfer/delayed status. Exploration taps are not errors. After a miss, preserve the response, show the relevant comparison, and explain the mechanism: “Among leaves, this shape is harder to spot. It is still a trait.” Wait for “Next case,” then test the same concept with fresh details. Corrections and replay remain unscored; award independent credit only on a subsequent probe. Correct-answer feedback should name the evidence used. The fresh-case-after-miss policy itself has not been validated by the cited research.

## Scientific accuracy requires separating benefit from adaptation

**A helpful effect does not establish evolutionary history.** Store observation, habitat, function, inheritance evidence, adaptation status, and source URL separately; never derive adaptation status from a “helps here” flag. Changing habitats does not erase a feature’s adaptation history. Never teach “trait OR adaptation,” “body part means inherited,” or “behavior means learned.” Environmental conditions can influence traits, including plant height ([Berkeley criteria](https://evolution.berkeley.edu/qualifying-as-an-adaptation/); [NGSS 3-LS3-2](https://www.nextgenscience.org/pe/3-ls3-2-heredity-inheritance-and-variation-traits)).

Include a useful human-made helmet contrast and a genuinely correct “Need more clues” case. Avoid individuals growing inherited features because they need them, generic “feathers evolved for flight,” and unsourced species/function claims. If depicting evolutionary change, show existing variation across generations, not intentional transformation ([NGSS 1-LS1-1](https://www.nextgenscience.org/pe/1-ls1-1-molecules-organisms-structures-and-processes); [Berkeley criteria](https://evolution.berkeley.edu/qualifying-as-an-adaptation/)).

## Conclusion

The first release should prioritize a small, scientifically verified case set over animal breadth. Pilot with short pre/post probes and an unseen transfer case, separating conceptual understanding from reading difficulty, touch errors, and adult help. The decisive question is whether children can explain what a feature does, where it helps, and why it remains a trait—not whether they finish the journey.
